To "know your nine generations" (*yesön üyeee medekh*) is a traditional Mongolian duty: remember the ancestors and pass their story on. In the most common reading, **nine generations = yourself + four generations up + four generations down**.

## The nine generations

| Gen. | Mongolian | Who |
|---|---|---|
| +4 | **Khulants** | Parents of a great-grandparent |
| +3 | **Elents** | Great-grandparents |
| +2 | **Övöö / emee** | Grandparents |
| +1 | **Etseg, ekh** (aav, eej) | Parents |
| 0 | **Öröö** — yourself | — |
| −1 | **Khüü, okhin** | Son, daughter |
| −2 | **Ach, zee** | Grandchildren — *ach* through a son, *zee* through a daughter |
| −3 | **Guch** | Great-grandchildren |
| −4 | **Jich** | Great-great-grandchildren |

> Sources name the generations differently, especially the lower ones (*guch, jich, döch, tach…*). Ask your elders how your family and region count them.

## Why it matters

- **Exogamy.** Traditionally people of the same paternal *yas* ("bone", lineage) did not marry within close generations. Knowing nine generations helped avoid close-kin marriage.
- **Clan names.** For most of the 20th century clan names (*urgiin ovog*) fell out of use and many genealogies were lost. Since the late 1990s Mongolians have been re-registering their clan names.
- **A legacy for children.** Knowing the ancestors' names, homelands and stories strengthens a child's sense of who they are.

## How to start

1. Start with yourself, then parents, grandparents and great-grandparents. Unknown dates can be entered as **"about 1920"**.
2. Record **both** your father's and mother's sides — the maternal side is often forgotten first.
3. Interview elders and **record their voices**.
4. The **Kinship** tab of your tree shows how many generations you have recorded so far.
