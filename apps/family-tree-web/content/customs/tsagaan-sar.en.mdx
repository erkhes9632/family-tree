Tsagaan Sar ("White Month") welcomes the new year of the Mongolian lunar calendar and is the most important traditional holiday. Families travel to visit their elders, so it is above all a celebration of **kinship and respect across generations**.

## Bituun — New Year's Eve

On the last evening of the old year the family cleans the home, settles debts and leaves bad things behind. Relatives gather for a generous meal — a full table on Bituun is believed to bring a full year. People stay home that night rather than visiting.

## The first day

- At dawn men **"mör gargakh"**: walk out in the direction the lunar almanac names for that year, often up a nearby hill or to an ovoo, to greet the sun.
- The family greets its **eldest member first**.
- Then everyone visits relatives, starting with the most senior households.

## How to greet properly (zolgokh)

1. The **younger person** extends both arms, palms up, and supports the elder's forearms from below — "I will support you".
2. The **elder** rests their arms on top, touches cheeks and says *"Amar baina uu?"* ("Are you at peace?").
3. Holding a **khadag** (blue silk scarf) during the greeting shows extra respect; its open edge faces the person greeted.
4. Spouses, and people of the same age, usually do not greet each other this way.

Men also **exchange snuff bottles**: offer yours with the right hand, palm up; the other person opens it slightly, smells it and hands it back.

## The festive table

| Dish | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Uuts** | Sheep's back with tail fat — abundance and respect |
| **Ul boov** | Stacked biscuits; the number of layers is **odd** (3, 5, 7) and reflects the age of the household's elder |
| **White foods** | Dried curds, cream, butter — purity of heart |
| **Buuz** | Steamed dumplings served to every guest |
| **Sweets** | Placed on top of the boov |
